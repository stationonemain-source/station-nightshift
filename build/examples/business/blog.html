How to shut off your water main

When a pipe lets go, the minutes before help arrives are what decide how much drywall and flooring you lose. Find your shut-off now, while nothing is wet.

1. Look for the house valve first

  1. Garage or utility closet. In most slab houses around here the main line comes up near the water heater. Look for a valve on the cold line before it branches.
  2. Where the line enters the house. Older homes with a crawl space often have it just inside the foundation, or outside near a hose bib.
  3. Turn it clockwise until it stops, or a quarter turn for a lever valve, so the handle sits across the pipe.

2. If there's no house valve, use the meter

The city meter box sits near the street, usually by the sidewalk or driveway. Lift the lid carefully (spiders and wasps like it in there). The valve on the city side of the meter turns a quarter turn with a meter key or an adjustable wrench.

Then open a faucet at the lowest point in the house to drain the pressure, and turn off the water heater if the leak is on the hot side.

Valve stuck or crumbling? That's a common call, and a $110 fix. Better to find out now than at 2 a.m.

Why your toilet keeps running

A toilet that runs or refills by itself can waste a couple hundred gallons a day. It's almost always one of three things, and you can check them with the tank lid off.

  • The flapper. Old rubber warps and stops sealing. Put a few drops of food coloring in the tank; if color shows up in the bowl without flushing, it's the flapper.
  • The chain. Too short and it holds the flapper open. Leave about half an inch of slack.
  • The fill valve. If water is spilling into the overflow tube, lower the float. If it still runs, the valve is worn.

Flappers and fill valves are cheap parts. If you'd rather not, we replace both in one visit, from $120.

A spring check for your fence

Oklahoma wind finds the weak post. Ten minutes of walking the fence line each spring saves you a whole section on the ground after the first big storm.

  1. Push each post. If it moves at the ground, the wood has likely rotted where it meets the soil or concrete.
  2. Look at the rails. Split rails or rusted-through nails at the posts let panels sag.
  3. Check the gate. If it drags or won't latch, the hinge post is usually leaning, not the gate.

One bad post can usually be replaced without touching the rest of the run. That's how most of our fence jobs start, from $175.
