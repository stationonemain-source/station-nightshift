The Butterfly Ranch audit

A working example of what we do before we build anything: forty minutes walking the customer's path, then an hour in the registries, then a second pass a week later on the one question that pays the bills — what happens when somebody tries to give them money. Every claim below is checked against a primary source, dated, and where something couldn't be verified, it says so.

Part one · What a stranger sees in forty minutes

The surface audit

Phone in hand, incognito, no special tools — the same walk any customer takes. The rule is evidence only: a claim needs a URL, a screenshot or a timestamp, or it doesn't get written down.

F-01

Google tells everyone they're permanently closed

The Maps listing reads "Permanently closed" — and there are two duplicate listings, both closed, at neighbouring addresses on the same road. Meanwhile the ranch is actively running summer camps and posting them on Instagram. Every stranger who searches for a horse rescue in Norman is being told this organization no longer exists.

Evidence · Google Maps search "Butterfly Ranch Norman", 12:20p Aug 26 — cross-checked against current-season Instagram activity.
F-02

You cannot give them money

The Donate button leads to a page that asks you to phone the founder. No form, no payment link, no giving levels — and across the entire site there are zero input fields and zero tappable phone numbers. For a rescue that runs on donations, the donation path doesn't exist.

Evidence · butterflyrescueranch.org/donate, 12:17p Aug 26 — page source contains no form, no payment link, no mailto. Correction: an earlier draft of this audit said donations "ride on a PayPal path" — that was wrong. The PayPal strings in the page are the site-builder's own feature flags, not a payment integration. Re-checked and corrected here.
F-03

The channels are islands

Instagram (1,072 followers) and Facebook (284) both carry the website link. The website links back to neither, and captures no email anywhere. Traffic flows one way into a page that can't do anything with it — the textbook definition of a dis-integrated system.

Evidence · Both profiles read logged-out, Aug 26 — follower counts taken from the profiles themselves, not a search result.
F-04

Search can't read the page

The browser-tab title is "Home | Butterfly Ranch" and there's no meta description on any page — so the words "Norman," "horse rescue" and "donate" never reach a search engine, and pasting the link into a text message produces a naked URL instead of a preview. The tab icon is still the site-builder's default.

Evidence · Page source of /, /about and /donate, Aug 26 — no description tag on any of the three.
F-05

The mission is already the asset

Not everything is broken. Rescued horses, a real kids' program, genuine camp photography, a 5.0-star rating, and a story no competitor in this market can copy. The site itself loads in about a second and reads fine on a phone. The problem isn't the product — it's the plumbing.

Evidence · Site loaded at 390px, ~1s; Google rating 5.0 from 6 reviews; program described on their own About page.

D-01

The buried asset: a real charity that never says so

We verified the tax exemption directly against the IRS Business Master File. Every gift to this organization is tax-deductible, and corporate matching programs would double some of them. Then we searched their entire public presence for that fact and found it nowhere — not the website, not the Donate page, not either social bio. A supporter literally cannot find the number their employer's matching-gift form asks for. They paid the full cost of becoming a charity and collect none of the return.

Evidence · IRS Business Master File (via ProPublica Nonprofit Explorer API), exemption ruled Jan 2024 · full-text search of site + both social bios, Aug 26.

D-03

Money she's eligible for and never asked for

Two funders whose published criteria she matches. One is local, funds animal wellbeing in Central Oklahoma, reviews small grants monthly, and states a vision of making Oklahoma the most humane place for animals — a kill-pen rescue serving underprivileged kids is that brief, verbatim. The other is national, gives $500–$5,000 for exactly her cost centers (vet, farrier, feed), and uses a free transparency profile as the application itself. Current grant revenue is zero — not because she was turned down, but because the prerequisite paperwork was never filed.

A for-profit neighbour already markets equine-assisted learning and therapy partnerships. The ranch has the charity status that schools and referral partners prefer and an instructor pursuing graduate study in equine-assisted counseling — the defensible version of that same offer, sitting completely unmarketed.

Evidence · Competitor site positioning + the ranch's own staff bios, Aug 26.
D-05

A local story with zero coverage

We searched for press and found none — not one article, ever. Consider what an assignment editor is being offered: kill-pen horses rehabilitated by a young founder who still competes in rodeo, paired with kids who may end up owning the horse they healed, with a summer of photography already shot. One pitch email is plausibly worth more reach than the ranch's entire social history.

Evidence · Press search across news and local outlets, Aug 26 — no results.
Part three · Why the money doesn't arrive

The donor engine

The first two parts asked whether a stranger can find them and what the registries know. This one asks the only question that pays the feed bill: a person decides to give — what happens next? Re-verified from the live site on September 2, 2026, a week after the audit above. Nothing had changed, and the whole site is still three pages long.

Walked as the class walks it — attention, interest, desire, action — plus the stage the framework leaves out, which for a charity is the one that compounds:

StageWhat a donor needs hereWhat exists today
AttentionTo be findable the moment someone decides to care about horses Google tells searchers they are permanently closed (F-01). No pixel and no analytics, so an audience can never be rebuilt or reached twice (G-07).
InterestOne sentence saying who this is and why it exists An 87-word homepage and no mission statement anywhere on the site (G-04).
DesireProof it works — a number, a face, an outcome Zero impact figures (G-05). Not one rescued horse is named, pictured with its history, or followed to adoption (G-06).
ActionA place to give, in under a minute, on a phone 56 words instructing you to telephone a person — on a number that isn't even tappable (G-01). Nowhere does it say the gift is tax-deductible (G-02).
After the giftA receipt, a thank you, and a reason to give again Nothing fires, because there is no list to fire it from — zero forms and zero email fields across all three pages (G-07).

Every stage fails, and every failure is free to fix. That is the finding: this is not an organization that needs money to become fundable. It is a fundable organization with no plumbing attached.

G-01

Donations have no address

The Donate page is fifty-six words long, and the operative sentence is "please contact Braxton Brackin for more information." There is no form, no giving platform, no suggested amount, no monthly option, no receipt. The only route to giving money is to telephone the founder — and the number appears as plain text, so on a phone it can't be tapped, only copied by hand. A donor arriving at the exact moment of intent is asked to place a cold call and wait for someone to pick up.

The fix is an afternoon: one hosted giving page with three suggested amounts anchored to real costs, a monthly option, and automatic receipting. Suggested amounts are not decoration — a blank field asks the donor to decide what the work is worth, and most resolve that by leaving.

Evidence · butterflyrescueranch.org/donate rendered and read Sept 2, 2026 — 56 words, zero form elements, zero email inputs, zero tel: links sitewide. The string "paypal" in the page source is Wix platform boilerplate (specs.stores.HideBillingFormForPayPalAndManualNotBrazil), not a payment path — the same false lead this audit corrected once already.
G-02

A registered charity that never says "tax-deductible"

They are a 501(c)(3) public charity, ruled January 2024, EIN 92-2460729. Across all three pages of the website the phrase "tax-deductible" appears zero times. So does "501(c)(3)". So does the EIN. So does the word "nonprofit."

This is the single most persuasive sentence a US charity is allowed to write, and it is the thing a donor checks before a gift large enough to matter. Right now a stranger reading this site has no way to tell it apart from a private riding barn asking for money — which is exactly what it looks like. Two lines in the footer and one on the Donate page close it, at no cost, today.

Evidence · Full text of /, /about and /donate read Sept 2, 2026 — no match for "tax-deduct", "501(c)(3)", "nonprofit", or any nine-digit EIN pattern. Exemption confirmed against the IRS Business Master File via the ProPublica Nonprofit Explorer API, same day.
G-03

Nobody can answer "how much of my money reaches a horse?"

It is the first question a serious donor asks and the first document a grantmaker requests. The ranch cannot answer it — there are still zero annual returns on record, re-checked today. No 990, no published budget, no split between program and overhead, no cost per horse per month.

The widely-cited bar is the BBB Wise Giving Alliance standard: at least 65% of total expenses spent on program activities. A three-person operation where the founder teaches, the barn manager runs the barn, and the horses eat most of the budget is very probably well above that line. That is the point — they are likely excellent and have no way to prove it, so the number gets assumed instead of read. A one-page budget and a filed return turn a private guess into a public asset.

There is a deadline attached, too. Three consecutive missed filing years revoke the exemption automatically, and revocation would take G-02 down with it — the tax deductibility they aren't currently using would stop being true before they ever start.

Evidence · IRS Business Master File via ProPublica Nonprofit Explorer API, read Sept 2, 2026 — exemption ruled Jan 2024, subsection 3, filings on record: 0. A 990-N e-Postcard can lag public mirrors, so this is a flag to confirm with the founder, not a finding against her. Program-expense threshold: BBB Wise Giving Alliance Standard 8.
G-04

There is no mission statement

Not a weak one. Not a buried one. The word "mission" does not appear anywhere on the website. The closest thing that exists is a sixty-word paragraph on the homepage describing the program mechanically — horses come from dangerous situations, they are rehabilitated, students are assigned one, students may adopt.

That paragraph contains a genuinely rare idea and states it like a procedure. This is the exact pair to hold in mind alongside F-05: the mission is the strongest asset they own, and it has never been written down as a sentence. A mission statement isn't corporate furniture for a charity; it's the sentence a supporter repeats to somebody else, and every dollar this organization will ever raise travels through someone's paraphrase of it. Right now there is nothing to paraphrase.

Evidence · Case-insensitive search for "mission" across the rendered text of all three pages, Sept 2, 2026 — zero occurrences. Homepage body text totals 87 words including navigation and the footer address.
G-05

No numbers, so no scale

How many horses have been rescued? How many are here now? How many students have come through? How many adoptions? What does a month of feed cost? The site answers none of it. The only figures anywhere on it are biographical — twenty-seven years riding, rode since she was eight, six years on a drill team.

"We rescue horses from kill pens" without a number is a sentiment. The same sentence with two numbers in it is an emergency, a budget and a donation ladder at once — this many taken in since 2022, this much a month to keep one alive. Those figures already exist. They are in her feed receipts and in her head, and they have simply never been written down anywhere a donor can reach them.

Evidence · Full rendered text of all three pages, Sept 2, 2026 — no count of horses, students, adoptions or dollars appears anywhere on the site.
G-06

Not one horse has a name

The organization owns the best story in its category and tells none of it. A horse comes out of a kill pen, is rehabilitated over months, is handed to a teenager who cares for it all summer, and may leave with that teenager as its owner. Rescue, rehabilitation, apprenticeship, adoption — a four-act structure that arrives free with the business model, and almost nobody in this market can copy it.

On the site it is three sentences of abstraction. No horse is named. No before-and-after exists. No student is followed. Nobody is quoted. Donors don't give to categories; they give to a specific animal with a specific face and a specific bill. One named horse, photographed the week it arrived and again ninety days later, would outperform every other change in this document — and it costs a phone camera and ten minutes.

Evidence · Rendered text and imagery of all three pages, Sept 2, 2026 — no individual animal is named or profiled anywhere on the site.
G-07

Nobody who visits can ever be reached again

Across the whole site there are zero forms, zero email fields, zero newsletter signups, and no tracking of any kind — no Meta pixel, no Google Analytics, no tag manager, no TikTok pixel. Nothing loads that could measure or remember a visitor.

The consequences stack. They cannot tell whether ten people or ten thousand visited last month. They cannot show anything to the person who read the Donate page and hesitated — the cheapest audience in marketing, and they don't have it. They cannot email the parent whose kid came to camp last summer. Every visitor is anonymous and single-use, so every dollar spent driving traffic is spent again from zero next time.

It also means the sequence below cannot be built yet. An email flow needs a list; a list needs a field on a page. One email input is the prerequisite for everything that follows. One note on the way in: the day a pixel goes on the site is the day it needs a plain visitor notice — trivial at install, awkward to retrofit.

Evidence · All three pages loaded to network-idle Sept 2, 2026 with request logging for facebook.net, googletagmanager, google-analytics, gtag, doubleclick, tiktok, clarity and hotjar — zero matches on every page. DOM query for form, input[type=email] and tel: links — zero across the site. No consent or cookie notice present.

Where the leverage is. Six of these seven cost nothing but an afternoon — a giving page, a footer line, a mission sentence, a named horse, an email field, a filed return. The seventh, the numbers, costs one evening with her own receipts. Not one of them needs a redesign, a budget, or an agency. The distance between this organization and a fundable one is almost entirely typing.

The build · what fires when the money lands

The gift is the start
of the relationship

Almost every charity treats a donation as the finish line. It's the cheapest moment they will ever have — someone has just proved, with money, that they care. What happens in the next sixty seconds decides whether there's a second gift. This is the sequence we'd wire, and it runs itself.

  1. The instant the payment clears — the receipt. Automatic, inside seconds, carrying the legal name, the EIN, the ruling date and the line "no goods or services were provided in exchange for this contribution." Not a courtesy: the IRS requires a contemporaneous written acknowledgment for any single gift of $250 or more before the donor can deduct it, and a receipt that arrives by hand three weeks later is a receipt that never arrives.
  2. Same day — the thank you, which is not the receipt. A separate message in Braxton's voice, using the donor's name and naming what the money touched. The receipt is an artifact; this is the relationship. Sending one and calling it both is the most common mistake in the sector.
  3. Day three — where it went. One horse. Photographed. Named. The condition it arrived in. This is the message that turns a one-time giver into someone who tells another person, and it is the direct output of fixing G-06.
  4. Day thirty — the outcome. The same horse, a month on. Nothing sells the next gift like evidence of the last one, and nobody else in that donor's inbox is doing it.
  5. Day sixty — the ask, converted. One invitation to move from a single gift to a monthly sponsorship at feed-bill price points. Someone who has watched an outcome converts at a rate a cold donor never will, and recurring gifts are the difference between a rescue that plans and a rescue that hopes.
  6. Forever — the list. The donor is on it, tagged by what they gave to and how much. Next spring's appeal, the camp announcement and the year-end letter now have somewhere to land, and the whole sequence runs again without anyone touching it.

All of that is one automation and one email field. The trigger is a payment event; the rest is six messages written once. It's the same machine we run for paying clients, pointed at a donation instead of an invoice — and for an organization whose entire donor experience currently ends at "call the founder," it's the difference between a gift and a supporter.

What we'd do about it

Ninety days, in order

Sequenced deliberately — each step is a prerequisite for the next, and nearly all of it is free. The expensive-looking problems turn out to be switch-flipping.

  1. Week one — stop the bleeding. Claim the Google listing, mark it open with real hours, merge the duplicate. Put the charity status and tax ID in the site footer and on the Donate page. Make the phone number tappable. Confirm the annual IRS filing is current — and if it isn't, file it before anything else on this list, because everything below depends on the exemption surviving.
  2. Week two — write the four things that don't exist. A mission sentence. Three impact numbers taken from her own records. One named horse with a photograph from the week it arrived. A one-page budget showing what share of a dollar reaches an animal. None of this is design work — it's an evening at a kitchen table, and every step after it is weaker without it.
  3. Weeks three and four — open the giving lane. Point the Donate page at one hosted giving page with three suggested amounts, a monthly option and automatic receipting. Switch the Facebook page to its nonprofit category to unlock a native donate button. Add one email field. Claim the transparency profile grant portals check. Install analytics and a pixel the same day, with a plain visitor notice.
  4. Month two — wire the sequence, then go to market. Turn on the six-message donation flow above. Publish the camp page at market-anchored tiers with sponsored seats funded by donors. Launch "sponsor a horse" at feed-bill price points. Put a QR review card in every parent's hand at camp pickup. Send the press pitch.
  5. Month three — compound. Submit the local grant letter of inquiry. Partner with a university service organization for volunteer days. Give the equine-assisted lane its own page, aimed at schools and counselors.

The one-paragraph version. "You already did the hard part — a real charity, a real program, and a story nobody in this market can copy. What's missing is plumbing: Google thinks you're closed, donors can't find your tax ID, and parents can't book the camp they'd happily pay for. Ninety days of switch-flipping, almost all of it free, connects what you built to the people trying to fund it."

How this was made

Method & honest limits

The surface audit took about forty minutes; the deep dive about an hour on top of it; the donor-engine pass another forty minutes a week later. No paid tools were used at any point — the whole thing is knowing which primary source answers which question.

  • Sources · IRS Business Master File · the organization's own website, read page by page · Google Maps listings · both social profiles read directly, logged out · competitors' published pricing pages · the funders' own grant criteria pages · and for part three, every page of the site rendered in a real browser with network logging, searched for giving mechanics, charity language, impact figures and tracking scripts.
  • Not claimed · Search-ranking position — an automated check was blocked partway, so no ranking claim appears anywhere in this audit. Whether the annual IRS filing is current is a question for the founder, not an assertion. The ranch's own camp pricing is unpublished, so it isn't stated. Public mirrors of IRS filings can lag a 990-N e-Postcard by months, so "zero filings on record" is reported as what the record shows, not as proof she failed to file. No impact number is invented anywhere in part three — where a figure would make the argument, the shape of the sentence is shown and the number is left for her to supply.
  • Corrected in public · An earlier draft claimed donations ran through PayPal. Re-checking showed those strings were the site-builder's own feature flags. The corrected finding — that no payment path exists at all — is worse for the organization and better as an audit.
  • Standing · This is unsolicited analysis of a public-facing organization, produced as a demonstration. Nothing here is a client engagement, and no non-public information was used.
